## What it is

You created a place where other people did AI safety work. Constellation's
governance guidance counts being "engaged in AI safety community building work,
such as convening, volunteering at, or participating in a reading group,
conference, workshop, sprint, or policy/strategy/governance research project."
Note that the published bullet spans a wide range from "participating" to
"convening" — those are not the same evidence, and the gap between them is
where most applicants sit without realising it.

## What strong looks like

- You ran it, not attended it. Set the curriculum, recruited the people, held
  the schedule.
- It survived past the first few sessions. A reading group that ran twelve weeks
  with attendance holding is evidence; one that ran three is a start.
- There is an artifact: the syllabus, the session notes, the sprint outputs, a
  public repo. Something that outlived the meetings and that someone else has
  since reused.
- You can say what came out of it — someone applied to a program, a project
  started, a writeup got published. Named outcomes, not attendance counts.
- You handled the unglamorous parts and can describe them: what you cut when
  attendance dropped, how you handled the participant who dominated discussion,
  what you would do differently.
- Facilitation, if you facilitated, was real work — you prepared, you had
  questions ready, you did not let the session become a lecture.
- Someone who was not you can vouch, and you can name them.

## What weak looks like

- Attending a reading group and listing it under community building. It is on
  the published list, and it is the floor of that list.
- Founding a university AI safety group that met twice and has no artifact.
- Volunteering at a conference for a day. Real, small, do not lead with it.
- Being in a Slack. Membership is not engagement.
- A group whose whole activity was reading the BlueDot curriculum together with
  no discussion outputs — that is `c10`, counted once, not twice.
- Counting the same activity under three criteria with different framings.
  Reviewers notice, and it reads as padding a thin record.
- Organising something with no evidence anyone attended.

## How to produce it

1. Do not found a new thing if a nearby thing exists and needs a facilitator.
   Taking over an existing group's curriculum is more credible and much faster
   than starting from zero.
2. If starting: pick a fixed-length format — eight or twelve weeks, stated up
   front. Open-ended groups die and leave no artifact.
3. Choose a curriculum that already exists (BlueDot's, a course reading list,
   the 80,000 Hours reading list) rather than writing your own for a first run.
4. Commit to publishing session notes from week one. This is what converts the
   activity into evidence; retrofitting it later is much harder.
5. Design one output the group produces together — a set of writeups, a shared
   literature review, a small sprint with a deliverable.
6. Track who does what afterwards. Outcomes are the strongest part of this
   criterion and they only exist if you stayed in touch.
7. Publish a short retrospective at the end, including what did not work.

## How a selector sees it

They look for a link and a verb. "Organised" and "facilitated" get read;
"participated in" and "attended" get skipped. If there is a syllabus or a notes
repo they open it to check it is real and see how long it ran. Under a minute.
What makes it skippable: no artifact link, or a verb that puts you in the room
rather than in charge of it. This criterion carries less weight than the writing
and research ones on the same track — it is corroborating evidence that you
build things with other people, not a substitute for having a position.
