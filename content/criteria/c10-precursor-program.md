## What it is

A structured program completed with something to show for it. Constellation's
technical-track guidance names SPAR, MATS, ARENA, and Pivotal as precursors, and
separately suggests you "take a BlueDot Impact course, or join a BlueDot Impact
research paper reading group." The programs differ enormously in selectivity —
BlueDot and self-study ARENA are open, MATS is not — and reviewers know the
difference. What survives that difference is the output.

## What strong looks like

- You finished, and the capstone or research output is public and linked.
- The output has been used, cited, forked, or discussed by someone unconnected
  to the program.
- You can say what you got wrong during it. "My first eval design had no control
  arm and my mentor caught it in week three" is worth more than a completion
  date.
- Work continued after the program ended — the project has commits, or a
  follow-up post, after the final week.
- Your specific contribution is separable from your cohort's, especially for
  team projects.
- If self-studying ARENA: your solutions repo is public, you implemented rather
  than read, and you can point at the exercise that took you three days.
- You can name the concrete skill it gave you and show where you have used it
  since.

## What weak looks like

- A completion certificate with no artifact. This is the single most common weak
  version on the whole list, and the reason "I already did that" and "that
  counts as evidence" come apart.
- The BlueDot capstone written for the deadline, never published, never
  revisited.
- ARENA "completed" by reading the material and running the provided solutions.
  The exercises are the content.
- A MATS or SPAR project listed with no link, no writeup, and no statement of
  what you did versus your mentor.
- Being currently enrolled, listed as if completed.
- Multiple partial programs. Two half-finished tracks read worse than one
  finished one.
- Listing the program as the achievement. The program is the container; the work
  is the achievement, and reviewers who have run these programs know that.

## How to produce it

1. Pick for the gap you actually have. ARENA if your implementation is weak,
   BlueDot if your safety-fundamentals framing is weak, SPAR or Pivotal if you
   need a mentored research cycle. Applying to all of them is not a strategy.
2. Decide the public artifact before week one, and tell your mentor that is your
   goal. Programs optimise for your learning; the artifact is on you.
3. Work the exercises. For ARENA specifically, the bench mirrors the curriculum
   in the `arena_3` track — use the section structure to keep pace and the
   implementation problems (attention, layernorm, KV cache, the debugging set)
   to check that you can write it without the notebook open.
4. Keep a friction log through the program. It becomes the honest retrospective
   nobody else writes.
5. Reserve the last two weeks for the writeup, not for more experiments. The
   unpublished capstone is the standard failure.
6. Publish within a month of finishing. After that it does not happen.
7. Do one follow-up piece three months later. That is what converts a completed
   program into a research thread.

## How a selector sees it

The program name buys you a few seconds of attention, weighted by how selective
they know it is. Then they look for the link. If there is no link, the line
reduces to "attended a thing," and given that these programs are themselves
capacity-bound, the reviewer already knows completion is partly a function of
who had a slot. What makes it skippable: no artifact, or an artifact that is
the program's assignment unmodified. Worth stating plainly: completing a
precursor program is common among applicants at final-round stage. It is close
to a floor rather than a differentiator, and if you have several and are still
being rejected, the missing evidence is more likely in `c01`, `c08`, or `c09`.
