## What it is

A visible track record of thinking in public over months. Constellation's
guidance asks applicants to "be active in AI Alignment Forum, posting and
commenting, or publishing original content," and to "run experiments on your
favorite open or closed source model and publish your methodology, data,
analysis, results, conclusions, and next research directions." The unit here is
not a post. It is a rate — evidence that you produce research output without
anyone assigning it.

## What strong looks like

- Six-plus months of dated output at a legible rhythm. Monthly is plenty;
  consistency beats volume.
- Each piece has a result, not a status. "Here is what I found and where it
  breaks" rather than "here is what I am learning."
- Visible improvement across the sequence. Later pieces are sharper, better
  scoped, better measured. Reviewers read the trajectory, not just the top item.
- You publish the ones that did not work, with the reason.
- Every experimental post carries the full set the guidance names: methodology,
  data, analysis, results, conclusions, next directions. That six-part shape is
  the difference between a post and a lab notebook entry.
- Substantive comments on other people's work — the ones that changed the
  author's mind, or got a real reply.
- Someone has responded, cited, or built on at least one of them.
- Next directions from an earlier post actually got followed up in a later one.

## What weak looks like

- One good post from fourteen months ago.
- Three posts published in the week before an application deadline. The dates
  are visible and the pattern is obvious.
- Reading notes and paper summaries. Useful to you, no signal — they show
  consumption, not production.
- "Thoughts on..." essays with no experiment and no falsifiable claim.
- Cross-posting the same piece to four platforms and counting it four times.
- Comments that are agreement, or that restate the post.
- A blog whose most recent post announces an intention to post regularly.
- Threads on social media. They are not durable, they are not findable, and a
  reviewer cannot skim them in the time available.

## How to produce it

1. Set a rate you can hold at your worst month, not your best. Monthly, or even
   six-weekly, held for a year, beats weekly abandoned in March.
2. Fix the shape in advance: question, method, data, result, what broke, next
   direction. A template removes the blank-page cost that kills cadence.
3. Mine what you are already doing. Bench work — a reproduction, an eval-design
   problem, a debugging problem you got wrong and understood — converts into a
   short post with an hour of extra effort. Use the bench's journal and reflect
   surfaces to catch these while the detail is fresh.
4. Keep each post small enough to finish in a weekend. The main failure mode is
   scope, not motivation.
5. Publish where it is durable and findable: Alignment Forum, LessWrong, or your
   own site with dated posts.
6. Comment substantively on two posts a month. Pick posts where you have
   actually run something adjacent.
7. Close the loop — every third post should pick up a "next direction" you left
   open, which is what makes the sequence read as an agenda rather than a feed.

## How a selector sees it

They open your blog or forum profile and look at the dates in the sidebar before
reading anything. A steady column of dates says "this person produces research
output unprompted," which is close to the thing fellowships are actually
selecting for. Then they read the most recent one. Twenty seconds for the
pattern, a minute for the post. What makes it skippable: a cluster of dates
right before deadlines, or a top post that is a summary of someone else's work.
This is a slow criterion — it cannot be produced in the month before an
application, which is exactly why it carries weight.
