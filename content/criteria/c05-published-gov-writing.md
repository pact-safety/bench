## What it is

A public piece of governance writing that engages a specific open question in a
specific researcher's agenda, and takes a position on it. Constellation's
governance-track guidance asks applicants to "have engaged deeply with or have
published writing on AI safety and governance topics related to a research
mentor's work — ideally through a research program (e.g. ERA, GovAI) or think
tank, such as RAND, CNAS, CSET." The affiliation clause is the amplifier; the
engagement is the criterion. Unaffiliated writing that lands on a real question
still works — it just has to be sharper to be read.

## What strong looks like

- It names the researcher, the paper, and the specific unresolved question. Not
  "building on work at GovAI" but "X's compute-threshold proposal leaves open
  how Y is verified, and here is the case that Z."
- It takes a position that someone could disagree with, and names who would.
- The mechanism is specified to the level of implementation. Which body, under
  what authority, enforced how, at whose cost, with what failure mode when the
  regulated party is uncooperative.
- It engages the strongest counterargument in its own words, not a weakened
  version.
- Empirical claims are sourced to something a reader can check. Where the
  evidence is thin, you say the evidence is thin.
- It knows what has already been proposed. Governance has a large grey
  literature; re-proposing an existing mechanism unaware is the standard tell.
- Length is disciplined. A 2,000-word piece with one defended claim beats an
  8,000-word survey.

## What weak looks like

- "The risks of AI and what policymakers should do" — a topic, not a claim.
- Recommendations at the altitude of "governments should coordinate
  internationally," which no reader can act on or dispute.
- A summary of three papers with a paragraph of your own at the end.
- Position-taking with no cost accounting: proposals that are free, enforceable,
  and universally acceptable do not exist, and reviewers read their absence as
  inexperience.
- Reciting an existing think-tank position without saying it is theirs.
- A LinkedIn post about a news item. Timeliness is not depth.
- Naming a mentor's work in a covering letter while the writing itself engages
  nothing they have argued.

## How to produce it

1. Pick one researcher whose agenda you would actually want to work in. Read
   their last three-to-five outputs properly, including footnotes — that is
   where the open questions are stated most plainly.
2. Find the question they flag as unresolved and have not answered. Write it out
   in one sentence.
3. Map the existing proposals on that question before writing. Use the bench
   knowledge base and Mira to trace who has argued what; then read the primary
   sources.
4. Write the strongest version of the position you disagree with, first. If you
   cannot, you do not yet understand the disagreement.
5. Draft your claim, then specify the mechanism until a lawyer or an engineer
   could tell you it will not work. That specificity is the whole difference.
6. Get one adversarial read from someone in the area before publishing.
7. Publish somewhere findable — Alignment Forum, a Substack you actually
   maintain, or a program's publication channel if you have one — and send it to
   the researcher with a two-sentence note.

## How a selector sees it

They read the title and the first two paragraphs, looking for a claim and a
named interlocutor. If both are present they skip to the mechanism section to
see whether it survives contact with implementation detail. Roughly a minute.
What makes it skippable: an overview framing, a recommendations list at national
or global altitude, or engagement with "the literature" in the abstract rather
than with somebody in particular. Reviewers in this track read a large volume of
competent, unfalsifiable prose; being disagreeable-with is the differentiator.
