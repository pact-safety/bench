## What it is

You have read the field's foundational material closely enough to argue with it.
Constellation's guidance for all applicants: "Read multiple classic AI safety
books and essays, such as The Alignment Problem, Human Compatible,
Superintelligence," and separately, "do a literature review on a topic in AI
safety you want to learn about, and summarize your results, insights, and next
steps." The second is how the first becomes visible — reading leaves no trace
until you write something only a close reader could write.

## What strong looks like

- You can say where two canonical sources disagree, and which you think is right
  on that point.
- You know which parts have aged badly, and why — a decade-old argument written
  before instruction-tuned models has load-bearing assumptions that no longer
  hold, and saying which ones is the demonstration.
- The literature review has a scope and a conclusion, not a bibliography. "Here
  are eleven papers on X, here is the disagreement they cluster around, here is
  the gap, here is what I would run next."
- The next-steps section is specific enough that you could start Monday.
- You separate what is established from what is argued. Much of the canon is
  argument; treating it as settled fact is the tell.
- Recent primary literature too — the canon is the base, not the frontier.
- It shows up unprompted in how you frame problems, rather than as a reading
  list you produce when asked.

## What weak looks like

- A list of books read. Nobody can verify it and nobody tries.
- Summaries that could be written from the Wikipedia article or a podcast
  episode. Reviewers can spot secondhand reading quickly, because secondhand
  sources emphasise different things than the text does.
- Treating the canon as scripture — quoting Bostrom as authority rather than as
  an argument with premises.
- A literature review that is an annotated bibliography with no synthesis and no
  position.
- Reading only one intellectual tradition. If you have read the LessWrong canon
  and none of the ML-community or governance-community critique, that is legible
  and it is read as insularity.
- "Currently reading" as a credential.
- Depth claimed but never demonstrated in any public writing.

## How to produce it

1. Read one book properly rather than four in summary. Take notes on where you
   disagree — that is the reusable output.
2. For each core claim, ask what would have to be true about current systems for
   it to hold, and check whether it does. Most of the value in re-reading the
   canon now is this test.
3. Pick a narrow topic for the literature review — narrow enough to read the
   primary sources in full, not a subfield.
4. Use the bench readings and knowledge base to build the citation map, and Mira
   to find the neighbouring work you missed. Then read the papers themselves;
   the map is scaffolding, not a substitute.
5. Structure the review as: question, what the field believes, where it
   disagrees, what nobody has checked, what you would run.
6. Publish it. An unpublished review is study; a published one is `c09`
   material and an entry point to `c01`.
7. Deliberately read the strongest critic of your own position and write the
   steelman.

## How a selector sees it

Almost never assessed directly on paper — nobody audits a reading list. It shows
up in interview, in one follow-up question about something you cited, and in
whether your writing places itself correctly in the field. If you name-check a
book and cannot say what its weakest chapter argues, that lands badly and you
usually will not be told. Under a minute in conversation. What makes it
skippable, on paper, is that a list is unverifiable — which is the real point of
this criterion: convert reading into a published review, or it does no work for
you at all.
