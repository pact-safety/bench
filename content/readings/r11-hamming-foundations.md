Hamming's lecture series on "how to do important work" — recorded
late in his career when he was teaching at the Naval Postgraduate
School. The two foundational chapters are about *problem selection*
and *foundations of computation*, but the deeper subject is how
research engineers think about what's worth doing.

**Why read.** Most algorithmic content teaches you the algorithms.
Hamming teaches you the *epistemics* of algorithmic work — which
problems are worth solving, when to switch tools, why elegance
matters operationally. The bench's algo problems
([algo-validation-window](/problem/algo-validation-window),
[algo-token-lcs](/problem/algo-token-lcs)) are individually
unimportant; how you *approach* them — that's what Hamming is
about.

**What to take from it.**

1. **Ch.1 "Orientation":** the "great problem" framing — you have a
   limited research career; spend it on problems whose answers
   *matter* even if you fail. The user's research-engineering target
   roles (frontier labs, safety orgs, fellowships) all turn on this
   kind of taste.
2. **Ch.2 "Foundations of the Digital Revolution":** Hamming's
   first-principles derivation of why digital won. Less about
   algorithms specifically; more about *how to think about
   exponential trends in your work*. Compute scaling, model scaling,
   eval-cost scaling all reward the same kind of reasoning.
3. **The recurring Hamming question:** "What are the important
   problems in your field, and why aren't you working on them?"
   It's irritating to ask yourself. That's why it's useful.

**This is cross-cutting content.** Not "how to do BFS." Read it for
the texture of how a senior practitioner thinks. The platform tags
it `algorithmic` because the spirit is there, but the value is
broader.
